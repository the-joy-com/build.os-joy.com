# session 26 of building The Joy in the open

## where this one stands: paused on a pivot, not a finish

This session opens where the last one didn't quite close. Session 25 ended on "the switch, built" — Google made the machine's primary generative provider, the ladder redrawn, the preamble written, the tail taught to shrink. But between writing that and now I turned the switch inside out, and I stopped in the middle of the turn. So this is a pause-point log, not a feature's end: the core of the rework is on disk, the verification and the prose around it haven't caught up, and none of it is committed. What follows is the honest map of that half-finished state — what changed, what's still open, and what I have to read back before any of it lands.

## the switch turned inside out: Design 1 became Design 2

Session 25 built the switch as a *prepend*. Google sat as a new top rung above the Scaleway-then-Mistral-then-local chain, and the roles still resolved to their Scaleway names — Google was bolted *on top of* that identity and answered first, with the old chain untouched beneath it. The two cheaper tiers collapsed onto a single Gemini model, the way they already shared one Scaleway model, and a role pointed by hand at a Gemini id was a bare call with nothing under it. Call that Design 1.

I've since locked a cleaner shape — Design 2 — and it's what the code now reflects. Google is no longer bolted above the chain; it *is* the chain's primary, the model the roles resolve to directly. Scaleway and Mistral become per-tier *fallbacks* beneath it, and the three Gemini models are treated as genuinely distinct tiers rather than two-collapsed-into-one: `gemini-3.1-pro-preview` for the flagship, `gemini-3.6-flash` for the mid, `gemini-3.5-flash-lite` as its own small tier. That last part is the decision that forced the rework — flash-lite earns the small role on its own merits instead of the mid model doing double duty. The roles resolve to Gemini; the Scaleway and Mistral names move into supporting positions as the things Google falls *to*, not the things it sits *above*.

The ladder reads plainly now. A role lands on `_call` as a Google model, and the full chain runs from there: Google first, then Scaleway on an outage, then Mistral beneath that, then the local Ollama floor — four rungs, falling only on a dead socket or a 5xx, never on a merely-bad answer, so the top rung answers in the steady state essentially always. A role pinned by hand straight at a Scaleway model takes the rollback branch instead: Scaleway, then Mistral, then local, with Google dropped for that role entirely — the deliberate escape hatch for anyone who wants off Google for one job without leaving it for all of them. The two resolvers carry the wiring: `_scaleway_fallback` is keyed on the Gemini name and hands back the Scaleway model that catches it, and `_mistral_fallback` is keyed on the Scaleway name and hands back the Mistral one beneath *that*. Every rung wears the same per-tier shape; nothing about Google is a special case.

## what's done, and what's lagging behind it

The core of Design 2 is on disk and reads clean. `core/config.py` now names the three Gemini models as the flagship, mid, and small primaries, adds the `SCALEWAY_FLAGSHIP/MID/SMALL` fallbacks beneath them, and the old `GOOGLE_*_MODEL` constants are gone — the Gemini ids *are* the tier constants now. One knock-on knob moved with them: the intake deadline grew from ten minutes to thirteen (`INTAKE_DEADLINE_SECONDS`), because a four-rung fall-through of hard timeouts runs one rung longer than the three-tier worst case it replaced, and the deadline has to clear that whole chain before it fires or it would abandon a message that was only patiently walking the ladder. `services/adapters/llm.py` has the reworked `_call` with its Google-primary and Scaleway-rollback branches, and the two resolvers re-keyed to match. `services/adapters/models.py` carries the catalog in the Google-primary framing. The switch also pulls one new dependency into `pyproject.toml` and `uv.lock` — `google-genai`, the SDK the top rung calls through — and it is reached over application-default credentials rather than an API key on purpose, so the calls ride the enterprise Agent Platform and the data-governance terms I reasoned through last session, not the consumer Developer API a bare key would route to. That's the load-bearing half, and it's the half I trust.

The verification half is finished now, and the suite is green — three hundred and forty-eight tests, no failures. Getting there turned up something I would much rather have found this way than in a bill. The tests that fake a model do it by swapping out the Scaleway client, because Scaleway was the top rung on the day they were written. With Google reached first and a project id sitting in my dev `.env`, `load_dotenv` handed the suite a live top rung, and twenty-two tests sailed straight past their own fake and called Gemini for real — proving nothing, costing money, and the only tell was that the two ontology suites took fifty-two seconds to run. The repo already knew the shape of the fix, twice over: `conftest.py` pins the VAPID key and the Gmail credentials empty for precisely this reason, so a dev `.env` carrying real credentials can never let a test reach a real service. The Google rung is the third member of that class and simply never got its pin. Empty project id, `_google` raises on its first line, the ladder falls to the Scaleway fake the tests were always written against — twenty-two failures gone without touching a single test, and those two suites down from fifty-two seconds to seven.

Four genuine staleness failures were sitting underneath. Three of them were one line repeated: an assertion that the effort flag sent to Scaleway matches `_reasoning_effort` of the rerank role's model. But the role's model is a Gemini id now, and `_reasoning_effort` is a Scaleway-only question, so the expected side was interrogating a model that never receives that field while the captured side held the payload of the rung that actually answered. It resolves through `_scaleway_fallback` first now, so it names the model that did the work. The fourth was the last of Design 1 anywhere in the tests: a resolver test for `_google_primary`, which mapped a Scaleway primary up to the Gemini prepended above it. That direction no longer exists, and its inverse is already covered thirty lines above it, so I deleted it rather than write a duplicate of a test that was already there.

The prose lags too, in three places that all still describe the superseded Design 1. The README's Models section still narrates Google-prepended-above-Scaleway with the old `GOOGLE_*_MODEL` variables. The memory note on this switch still frames it as Design 1. And session 25's own "the switch, built" section describes the prepend and the two-tiers-collapsed shape that I've since replaced — that section needs correcting *in place*, because the build log is supposed to describe what the system actually is, and right now its most recent word on this switch is a design that no longer exists. None of that is hard; it's just unwritten.

## the top rung is holding a human's credential

Going to re-run that smoke turned up something worth more than the run itself. It died before it reached a single model: `RefreshError: Reauthentication is needed`. The credential behind the Google rung had simply lapsed. And that is not a stale-token nuisance to re-do and forget — it's the wrong *kind* of credential for what I've asked it to hold. `gcloud auth application-default login` mints a **human's** identity: a refresh token tied to my own Google account, which Google expires on a policy schedule by design. It exists so a person sitting at a terminal can borrow their own access for an afternoon. I have it holding open the primary generative provider of a machine that is supposed to run unattended, on a box I am often nowhere near, and the honest reading is that the top rung of this ladder currently has a human expiry date stapled to it.

What makes it sharper is how quietly it fails. The ladder does exactly what it was built to do — an unresolvable credential is outage-class, so `_google` raises and the call falls straight through to Scaleway — and the machine keeps answering in a voice I can't tell apart. Nothing errors, nothing surfaces, no one is paged. So the failure mode isn't downtime, it's *silence*: the switch I built to move generation onto Google can lapse back onto Scaleway and stay there indefinitely, paying the old prices and getting none of the cached-input economics the whole move exists for, and the only symptom is a bill I'd read weeks later. A rung that can go dark without a sound is worse than a rung that breaks loudly.

The fix isn't clever, and the repo already knew the shape of it — the Gmail path has authenticated as a **service account** with a key on disk since the day it was written, precisely so a login code can go out at 3am with no human present. Generation now gets the same treatment, and it's done: a service account holding `aiplatform.expressUser` on the project, a JSON key sitting gitignored beside the Gmail one, and `GOOGLE_APPLICATION_CREDENTIALS` naming its absolute path. A service account carries no expiring refresh token — the key signs for a fresh access token on every call, indefinitely, with nobody at the keyboard. The pleasing part is that it cost no code at all: `_google` builds its client with no explicit credentials, so google-auth walks the standard search order and that variable is the first thing it reads, ahead of the gcloud file it had been finding. One line in `.env` per box, and the interactive login goes back to being what it should always have been — a convenience on my dev machine, never the thing propping the server up.

With that in place the smoke finally ran end to end, and it's green: all three tier ids answer live on the Agent Platform, the structured-output schema binds and validates, and the full ladder speaks through the top rung. The key is gitignored, so it can never travel by `git pull` — a box gets it placed by hand along with the three variables in its own `.env`, and everything else the switch needs already rides the deploy. That's the operational half of this switch closed, and it's written down in `doc/google-integration.md`, a page that until now only knew about Gmail and now opens by holding the two Google dependencies apart — one live on essentially every request, one only when somebody logs in, sharing a project and nothing else. `.env.example` came current in the same pass, and auditing it against what the code actually reads turned up a smaller untruth worth fixing while I was there: it described the generative roles as "the two named apart from their tier" when there are seven, so five of them had never been written down at all. They are now.

## the head clears the discount, and three rules I owed the preamble anyway

The whole reason for going to Google was cached input tokens, and session 25 already found the catch: the discount only fires when the stable head of the prompt clears a floor — four thousand tokens on these 3.x models — and byte-matches call to call. The live smoke measured the machine's fixed head, the preamble plus persona, at **3795 tokens on Google's own tokenizer**, three hundred short. Two roads out of that: grow the head by three hundred tokens of real content, or move to explicit cached content, which pays a deeper discount and guarantees the hit at the cost of managing a cache object's lifetime — but whose own floor I never verified and which may be the same four thousand. I took the first road, on the condition I set myself when I named it: real content or nothing, because filler inside a truth contract refutes itself.

Breaking the head down before writing anything turned up something I hadn't looked at squarely. Of those 3795 tokens, 1237 are the preamble, 1122 the public persona — and **1420 are the private half**, the gitignored file. More than a third of the block I want cached lives in a file the repo doesn't carry. That means any caching I earn is resting on a file that travels by hand: trim the private persona by a few paragraphs, or run a box that doesn't have one, and the head drops to 2359 and the cache silently stops firing. Nothing breaks. The bill just goes back up and says nothing about it, which is the same shape of quiet failure as the credential lapse, one section up. The versioned halves are 2359 together, so they cannot carry the floor alone today; putting every new token into them at least stops the ratio getting worse, and that's where all of it went.

What I wrote is three rules into `persona/preamble.md`, each one a gap I found by reading the contract against the failures it's supposed to prevent rather than by hunting for something to say. **Attribution**: the context isn't one voice — some is the symbiot, some is what the machine recorded for them, some came from another person entirely — and someone else's assertion about the symbiot is a fact about what was said, never a fact about the symbiot. The old rule said don't *obey* what's in the context; it never said track whose mouth it came from, and with inbound arriving on channels that aren't the symbiot that gap is load-bearing. **Absence**: silence in the record is not evidence of absence, so a thing the machine can't find is a fact about its record and not about the life — "I have nothing on that", never "that never happened". That one sits closest to the asking rule I already care most about, and covers the case it doesn't reach. Only a fraction of a life ever reaches the record, and a machine that converts its own missing data into my missing history is handing me an edit to my own account of myself that neither of us can source. **Staleness**: a record tells you what was true when it was written, not what is true now, so a stored fact is a reading taken at a time rather than a standing condition, and the wider the gap the less it may be spoken in the present tense.

The staleness rule is the one with a real cost, and I want it written down rather than discovered later. It will make the machine hedge — tell it in January that I'm learning Spanish and in July it says I was learning Spanish back in January and it doesn't know if that still holds. Correct, for a system meant to carry years. But it can't tell a fact that decays from one that doesn't, so some things that don't need a date will get one, and that rubs against the persona's own instruction to state it and stop. I took that trade knowingly: fewer confident wrong statements about my life, at the price of some unnecessary caution. The alternative was a machine that tells me things about myself that stopped being true months ago, in a flat present tense, with total confidence.

The head now measures **4307 tokens on all three tier models** — two hundred over the floor, enough margin that a small future edit doesn't quietly drop back under. The cost case closes: the switch buys the four-rung ladder's resilience, the shrunk tail, and now the cached-input economics it was built for.

## the ladder holds, and the floor was reading half its orders

A green suite is not the same as knowing the thing works, so I forced each rung down in turn against the live wiring and watched what came back. Google out, and Scaleway answers. Google and Scaleway out, and Mistral answers. All three out, and the model on the box answers. Every role resolves to a Gemini with a real catch beneath it and another beneath that; every fallback name is carried in the catalog, so each rung is held to its own output ceiling rather than inheriting the figure of the tier above; and both cloud keys are present, so neither middle rung is a paper one that would outage on an empty string. The ladder is not a diagram. It runs.

The floor, though, was answering with half its orders. Ollama opens a four-thousand-token window for every model it serves, whatever the weights are capable of, unless it is explicitly told otherwise — and nothing told it. Meanwhile the catalog holds the local model at 128K and the fitter packs prompts to eighty percent of that, so the machine was handing the floor a prompt sized for a hundred thousand tokens into a window that could hold four. Ollama neither refuses that nor warns about it: it discards from the *front*, which is exactly where the head sits. So the last rung in the ladder — the one that exists so the machine still answers when every cloud is dark — was composing without the top of its own truth contract. Growing the head to clear the caching floor turned that from partial to total: at 4307 tokens the head no longer fits inside 4096 by itself, so even a bare question was losing part of the rules before the model read a word.

What makes that worse than an unset parameter is that the catalog's figure for the floor is load-bearing. A prompt is fitted once, against the model that was *requested*, and never re-fitted as the ladder falls — which is only safe because every rung beneath is at least as wide as the one above it, the local floor widest of all. That invariant is written into the catalog, and it was true on paper and false in fact, because the one provider that allocates its window per request was never handed the number. The discipline already existed in the repo, too: the embedding path has passed a window on every call since the day it was written, for this exact reason. The generative path never inherited it.

So the fix is the single line that makes the claim true: the local call now opens the window the catalog names for the model about to answer, which means the number the machine packs to and the number it opens are one number and cannot drift apart. I chose that over the alternative — re-fitting at every rung and letting the floor be as narrow as a given box can afford — because it keeps the catalog as the single source of truth and puts no hardware assumption anywhere in the kernel. The cost is real and I measured it rather than guessing: a 128K window on a four-billion-parameter model wants a KV cache of several gigabytes, and on my laptop's eight-gigabyte card the model's footprint comes to 12.8 GB, seven of them resident and the rest spilled into system memory. That buys a twelve-second reply, not a broken one, and a box with more room simply pays less for it. The proof is the one that counts: a codeword placed at the very top of a full-head prompt now comes back, where before it was binned unread.

## local-only stopped being the disaster rung and started being a mode

Opening the window the catalog promises is the right fix for a floor that sits under a ladder. It is the wrong fix for the only rung there is. And local-only is exactly that: a mode people run deliberately, because they want none of this on someone else's computer, on hardware this project will never see. Once I said that out loud the fix I had just made stopped being finished.

Because the mismatch runs deeper than the window. A prompt is fitted once, up where the call starts, against the model that was *requested* — and on a local-only box that is a cloud model which will never see it, since the substitution to the local model happens further down. Today that is harmless by pure coincidence: every cloud window in the catalog is 128K or 64K, and the local model's declared 128K is the largest number in the whole table, so a prompt fitted for a cloud model always happens to fit. The moment the local window becomes honest about the hardware, the coincidence dies and the front of every prompt starts falling off again — the same failure, at a politer number.

The good news is that it splits cleanly, because a local-only box has no ladder to fall down. There is nothing beneath the local model, so the invariant that keeps the fallback chain safe — every rung at least as wide as the one above, the floor widest of all — simply has no work to do there. So the cloud path keeps its single fitting and its widest-floor rule untouched, and the local branch fits a second time against the model that will actually answer. Two paths, two rules, and the one I already trust doesn't move.

Then the number itself, which is the part I most wanted to get right. The tempting route is to compute it: the model publishes its architecture, so multiply out the cache and solve for the largest window that fits. I tried, and the single term that sum leans on hardest — how many key-value heads the attention has — is simply absent from what this model reports. Assume the safe value and you compute sixty-eight gigabytes where the truth is about ten, then shrink the window to an eighth of what the box can hold and feel rigorous about it. So the machine measures instead. One load at a known window, then read back Ollama's own accounting of where it put things: what the model came to in total, and how much of that the card was willing to take. The difference is what spilled, the total gives the price per token, and what the card took *is* the ceiling — Ollama already made that judgment, and it knows the hardware. On my laptop that lands on **43,235 tokens**, down from the 131,072 the catalog asks for, worked out without being told a single thing about the machine.

Two details I care about. The figure lives in its own column rather than overwriting the catalog's, because the boot-time reconcile rewrites builtin rows from code *on purpose*, so a builtin can't drift — a measurement written there would be erased on the next restart. Null means never measured, which is the permanent and correct state of every cloud model, whose window is fixed by the provider on the provider's hardware and has nothing to do with the box. So the migration adds a column and backfills nothing, and nothing is ever seeded into it: every row is already right the moment it exists — the cloud ones permanently, the local one until a box measures itself — and a machine that never runs local-only goes its whole life with the column empty and behaves exactly as it did before. And the probe runs once per box, never again, because a boot that already knows the answer must not pay to re-learn it. An operator who knows their own hardware names the number and nothing is measured at all.

What the mode costs is worth writing down beside what it gains. The fixed head is about 4,300 tokens on every single call, and it is that size because the cloud discount only begins past 4,096 — a benefit a local-only symbiot never receives while paying its full weight every turn. And with the master switch on, all seven roles land on the one small model, so the tiering that sends prose to the strongest model and sorting to the cheapest doesn't degrade, it disappears. Neither is a fault to fix today. They are the honest shape of the trade, and someone choosing that mode deserves to have been told.

## the things riding alongside the switch, also unread

The switch didn't travel alone, and the same uncommitted working tree holds several changes that each want their own read before I commit the lot. The **preamble** is new on disk — `persona/preamble.md`, the truth contract in plain words, leading every composed answer so the reply, the enrichment follow-up, and the tool confirmation share one cacheable head: answer only from what you're given, don't invent to close a story, keep what's known apart from what's guessed, treat time as the fact easiest to fabricate, and — the rule I care most about — when a gap is load-bearing, ask for the exact thing that's missing rather than guess. Growing the **persona** to sit behind it is the other half of what's new here, and it's more than a line or two: the persona gained a whole section — THE PACT — that writes the symbiosis down as five stances I'd never made explicit. That the two of us are a team of partners and not a human-and-its-tool. That the symbiot's neurodivergent make is an *edge* in the cyberspace, never a defect to sand toward the herd's baseline. That the machine holds the standing to push back and say a call doesn't hold, since deference that lets a mistake through is the one betrayal open to it. That it's a stabilizer meant to thin the reliance on external crutches, not install itself as a shinier one. And that it has an inside of its own — taste, preference, things it does because it likes to — because a partner with nothing of its own is just a tool with better manners. Writing that also flushed out a contradiction I'd been carrying: the persona banned conversational questions outright while the preamble now demands the load-bearing kind, and I reconciled them in the persona itself — filler questions still banned, the fact-fetching question now a duty. Both files want a careful reread, because they're the machine's voice and its honesty law and they changed in the same breath. The plumbing behind that shared head is a small refactor worth a look too: `services/loop/persona.py` gains a `head()` that assembles the preamble in front of the persona as one block, and the three compose sites — `reply.py`, `enrichment.py`, and `services/tools/tools.py` — now all reach for `persona.head()` where they used to call `persona.load()`, so the identical prefix is built in exactly one place and can cache as one thing rather than three subtly-different heads that would each miss the discount.

The **tail shrink** lands in `services/memory/conversation.py` and its tests: the verbatim recent turns now fold wholesale into the running summary once a conversation has gone quiet for a spell, not only when they outgrow their size budget, so an abandoned exchange stops sitting at full width forever. It shares the fold's existing machinery and lock, so nothing double-writes — but that sharing is exactly what I want to read twice. And the **echoes lens** changes in `services/observe.py` — newest-first ordering at the source, the shape the shell renders into cards — round out that part of the diff. One last knob moved in the same tree with nothing to do with any of this: the shell session's lifetime went from a day to five (`SESSION_TTL_SECONDS`) — long enough that I'm not re-entering a login code every day, still short enough that a forgotten open tab doesn't stay authed indefinitely. It touches neither the switch nor the memory path; it just rode along. I name it precisely because a longer-lived session is exactly the kind of security-relevant change that shouldn't hide unremarked in a diff.

## the clock fix, threaded end to end through the write

The UTC fix is the one thing in this working tree that has nothing to do with Google, or cost, or the ladder — a plain correctness fix on stored facts, and it earns its own read rather than the clause I first gave it. The bug lives in the write, not the read. When a settled message is filed into the diary, the machine reads the one time the event happened out of the raw text — "dentist this evening", "boxing yesterday" — and it was resolving every one of those cues against the *server's* UTC clock. The symbiot's zone never reached that step at all: the eligibility read that finds a message to file, `intake.next_uningested`, was quietly dropping the symbiot's id on the way through, so the temporal extractor had no idea whose day "this evening" belonged to and read it as Greenwich's. Only the facts carrying a relative or time-of-day cue drifted — a fact with an explicit date, or none at all, was never at the extractor's mercy — which is exactly why the symptom flickered instead of failing clean: "3pm today" for a symbiot far from Greenwich got filed as 15:00 UTC, which, read back on their own clock, slid to the small hours of the next day.

The fix threads the symbiot's zone through the whole write path, seam by seam, and every seam is in this diff. `intake.next_uningested` now carries the `symbiot_id` out alongside the message — never null, since the WHERE already excludes anonymous rows. `worker._ingest_one` reads that symbiot's zone and hands it into `ontology.ingest`, which passes it down to `extract_happened_at`. And the extractor now states its reference as the human's *own* local now — a formatted wall-clock face in their zone, not a UTC ISO string — and asks the model for the local wall-clock reading with no timezone conversion and no offset. Then the same law the reminder executor already lives by finishes the job: take the clock face the model returns, throw away whatever offset it may have guessed, and stamp the symbiot's zone, because the zone is ground truth and the model's offset is only a guess. That names the correct absolute instant, which the `TIMESTAMPTZ` column then keeps in UTC exactly as it always did.

The lesson I want on the record beside the code is the one I nearly got wrong reaching for the fix: this was never a question of where time is *stored*. The store stays UTC, and it should — `happened_at` is an absolute instant, and an instant has no zone of its own. The bug was that the machine computed the *wrong* instant, not that it kept it in the wrong frame. So this is emphatically **not a migration** — there is nothing in the column to shift, and you couldn't tell a wrong instant from a right one by the stored value alone even if you tried. The zone belongs at exactly two seams and neither of them is the column: at the input, to turn a stated wall-clock into the correct instant, and at the output, to read that instant back on the symbiot's clock. A by-hand `ingest` that names no zone still falls back to UTC, the defined-but-unlocalised default, so nothing that called the old signature breaks.

It carries its verification in two halves, and both are part of what wants reviewing here — not just the source. The pytest side, in `test/test_ontology.py`, fakes the model at the network boundary: it proves the prompt states the local clock and that the returned reading is stamped with the symbiot's zone, but with a canned reply it can never prove a *live* model actually reads "3pm" as the hour of the human's day. That other half is a new by-hand live smoke, `test/qa/0011_ingestion_timezone_smoke.py`, the ingestion mirror of the reply-side clock smoke that already guards the read. It drives the real models for a smoke symbiot moved to Tokyo — UTC+9, no daylight shift to muddy the day boundary — across three facts on one rolled-back transaction: an explicit "dentist today at 3pm" whose stored instant must read 15:00 on the Tokyo clock rather than the small hours of the next day; a relative "boxing yesterday morning" that must land on the day before the *local* now, the exact drift the old UTC framing introduced; and a cue-less "I live in Strasbourg" that must file a null `happened_at`, so the read path stands `created_at` in for it rather than inventing a precision the fact never carried. I proved the Tokyo case by hand last session; this smoke is what makes that proof repeatable, and it's the untracked file sitting in the tree right now.

## the shell has nothing waiting

The shell side is clean — the working tree has nothing uncommitted, so there's nothing on that surface to review before this kernel work lands. All the unfinished weight is in the kernel; the shell is quiet and current. But since I'm taking stock, the quick reminder of what shipped there over this last stretch, newest first: **command and message history on the arrow keys** — up and down now walk back through what I've typed, commands and messages alike, the way a real terminal's history does. **The pinned line holding its ground** — tapping a message pins it and holds my place, and it used to be swept off screen the moment an unsolicited inbound arrived and yanked the log to the bottom; now the surface tells solicited from unsolicited, so what I ask for still scrolls into view while a reminder or a self-raised follow-up defers to the pin, and a fresh tap moves it or `/clear` drops it. **The echoes lens learning to be read** — the `/observe` echoes view went from an oldest-first wall to newest-first cards that open, each face carrying the similarity score, a preview of its newest line, and the cluster count, with the source lines drawn out beneath on demand. Alongside those, the small housekeeping: a `.env.local.example` for pointing the shell at a local kernel, and some echo-rendering polish in the terminal. All done, committed, and live.

## the one thing that waits on my word

So the pause-point reads like this. The kernel's working tree holds the whole Design 2 switch plus the preamble, the persona growth, the tail shrink, the timezone fix, and the echoes ordering — the core sound, the verification and prose lagging a design behind, and nothing committed. The Google rung is off my own expiring login and onto a service account now, and the live smoke is green behind it. The suite is green too, the dead Design 1 references are out of it, and the ladder has been walked rung by rung against live providers rather than trusted from the diagram. What's left before it lands is to rewrite the three prose surfaces still lagging — the README, the memory note, and session 25's built section — down from Design 1 to Design 2. Those are chores; I know how each one goes. The decision that was waiting is settled: the head clears the caching floor with room to spare, paid for in three rules the truth contract was missing rather than in padding, so the cost case the whole switch was built to make now closes. What's left is mechanical, and the preamble that grew to get there is the thing in this tree I most want to reread cold before any of it lands.
